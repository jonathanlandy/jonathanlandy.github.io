LTV and marketing

Published on Wed 20 May 2026

I

Let

\begin{equation} y_i = m x_i + \epsilon_i \tag{1}\label{1} \end{equation}

where \(\epsilon_i\) is noise with variance \(\sigma^2\). If we choose the scale such that our \(x\) samples satisfy \(E(\delta x^2) = 1\), we have

\begin{equation} E(\delta y^2) = E\!\left[(m\,\delta x_i + \epsilon_i)(m\,\delta x_i + \epsilon_i)\right] = m^2 + \sigma^2 \tag{2}\label{2} \end{equation}

and

\begin{equation} E(\delta x \cdot \delta y) = E\!\left[\delta x \cdot (m\,\delta x_i + \epsilon_i)\right] = m \tag{3}\label{3} \end{equation}

The covariance matrix is then

\begin{equation} C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & m \\ m & m^2 + \sigma^2 \end{pmatrix} \notag \end{equation}

The eigenvalues are

\begin{equation} \lambda_{\pm} = \frac{1 + m^2 + \sigma^2 \pm \sqrt{(1 + m^2 + \sigma^2)^2 - 4\sigma^2}}{2} \tag{4}\label{4} \end{equation}

For small noise, the larger eigenvalue is

\begin{equation} \lambda_+ = 1 + m^2 + \frac{m^2}{1+m^2}\,\sigma^2 + O(\sigma^4) \tag{5}\label{5} \end{equation}

The corresponding eigenvector is

\begin{equation} v_+ = [m,\; \lambda_+ - 1] \tag{6}\label{6} \end{equation}

In the low \(\sigma^2\) limit, this is

\begin{equation} v_+ \sim \left[m,\; m^2 + \frac{m^2}{1+m^2}\,\sigma^2\right] \tag{7}\label{7} \end{equation}

In PCA, we project the data onto this vector. This has slope

\begin{equation} \text{svd slope} = \left(m^2 + \frac{m^2}{1+m^2}\,\sigma^2\right)/m = m\!\left(1 + \frac{1}{1+m^2}\,\sigma^2\right) \tag{8}\label{8} \end{equation}

Relative to the regression slope \(m\), this is increased by a scale factor of

\begin{equation} \kappa \equiv 1 + \frac{\sigma^2}{1+m^2} \tag{9}\label{9} \end{equation}

Now, the slope is related to the angle via

\begin{equation} m = \tan(\theta) \tag{10}\label{10} \end{equation}

So to get the change in angle, we use

\begin{equation} \theta_2 - \theta_1 = \arctan(\kappa m) - \arctan(m) \approx \frac{m}{1+m^2}(\kappa - 1) + O(\kappa - 1)^2 \tag{11}\label{11} \end{equation}

The change in angle is then

\begin{equation} \Delta\theta = \frac{m}{(1+m^2)^2}\,\sigma^2 + O(\sigma^4) \tag{12}\label{12} \end{equation}

The coefficient in front of \(\sigma^2\) is \(\frac{m}{(1+m^2)^2}\), and this is largest at \(m = 1/\sqrt{3}\), which means \(\theta = 30\) degrees.
